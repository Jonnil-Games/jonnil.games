Okay, so—is there such a thing as "perfect game design"? A "one true way" to make games? Nah, not really. I'm not a perfect game designer, but I _do_ have some principles that I stick to. These are things I’ve figured out through trial, error, and a lot of joyful tinkering on my own game design journey.

I used to think mechanics were _the_ core of a great game—that if you nailed the gameplay, everything else would fall into place. But the more I’ve made games, the more I’ve realized: it’s not just about mechanics. You need visuals, you need sound, and most importantly, you need to understand how something _feels_.

This topic goes _deep_, and I’d love to explore it more in future posts. But for now, here’s a peek at some of the ground rules we follow at Jonnil Games.

## Accessibility

I want everyone to feel welcome playing my games. For me, that means:

- Simple, comfy controls
- Clear menus
- Adjustable difficulty

Nobody should feel left out. I’ll be the first to admit I’m still learning how to fully embody this principle in practice.

Take _Sticky Paws_, for example. It nails the simple controls—I’m proud of that! And it has adjustable difficulty... sort of. The issue is that the difficulty options lean _really_ hard into "easy mode" without offering a proper challenge for players who want one. That's something I want to do better in the future.

Same with the menus. They started out nice and clean, but over time, updates made them more cluttered and harder to navigate. It’s something I try to be conscious of now. One idea I’ve had is unlocking menus over time to ease players in gradually, instead of dumping everything on them at once.

Which brings me to...

## Keep it understandable!

Cognitive load is real, y’all. I always ask myself: how much brain energy is this game asking for?

Games should teach you naturally, without overwhelming you. The best feeling is when things just _click_—when you intuitively understand the challenge and feel empowered to take it on.

Have you ever played a game where you didn’t even know what the challenge _was_? I’m not talking about a slow reveal of mechanics (which can be fun!), but more like: you literally can’t tell what you're supposed to be doing. That feels _bad_. So I try to structure challenges so that they make sense as they unfold. Clarity matters.

Also, I have to be careful—sometimes things I _didn’t_ mean to be a challenge end up becoming one! If I make the menus too convoluted, suddenly _that_ becomes the hard part... and not in a good way. That kind of unintentional challenge isn’t fun—it’s frustrating. It’s not like level design where you can control the experience. So I try to take a step back and ask myself:

> “Is this introducing itself to new players in a good way? Or am I making things harder than they need to be?”

## About our older games

Some of my earlier projects don’t follow all of these ideas. Even _Sticky Paws_ misses the mark in places. And that’s okay! I’m still figuring things out. It’s all part of the journey, and I’m proud of every game I’ve made—even the janky ones.

I could ramble for ages about game design (and I will, just you wait!), but I wanted to keep this post relatively short—just a little something to start fleshing out the blog. I’ll definitely go deeper into these topics in the future.

I hope you found this interesting, even if it’s just a surface-level dive into my design brain. It’s good to revisit these core principles now and then. When you’ve been making games for a while, it’s easy to fall into the trap of thinking, “I know what I’m doing.” But that mindset? That’s how you become _arrogant_! Don’t do it!!

Keep making games. Keep learning. Stay humble. <3

— Jonna
